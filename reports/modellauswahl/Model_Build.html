STEP 01

Research question and current model

What are we trying to estimate?

Our project investigates how regional structure, especially urbanisation, relates to reported COVID-19 incidence in German districts, and whether that association varies by age and time. Reported cases are not all infections; these ecological data do not establish individual risk or causal effects.

Project record: 400 harmonised districts, 209 ISO weeks (2020–2023), ages 0–79 and pooled 80+. The eight-wave analysis covers 132 weeks and about 98% of reported cases, not the full observation period (§§1, 4).

STEP 02

Weekly vs. wave panel

Which observation unit and exposure match our research question?

Phase 2 / Gesamtbericht §5: W1 has 40.7% zero weekly cells and 73.4% below five cases; aggregation reduces sparsity but loses within-wave timing. Weekly data do not require one coefficient per week: weekly baseline plus phase-level interactions is another possibility (already supported in principle by model_formulas.R).

MeasureCalculationMeaning
Weekly incidence10 / 10,000 × 100,000 = 100 (week 1)Rate in a specific week
Cumulative wavesinzidenz100 / 10,000 × 100,000 = 1,000Total reported burden over 4 weeks
Person-week rate100 / (4 × 10,000) × 100,000 = 250Average weekly intensity (exposure-weighted)

Interpretation. The wave panel measures total burden or average weekly intensity, depending on exposure. Weekly data preserve timing. IRR (incidence rate ratio) compares two rates, not their absolute difference. With equal complete duration within each wave and unrestricted wave intercepts, the two wave offsets yield the same within-wave IRRs; absolute rates differ.

Technical notes and additional evidence
E = Σ weekly population = T × mean weekly population
log(E) = log(mean population) + log(T)

If every row in a wave covers the same T weeks, changing offsets shifts the wave intercept only. With freely estimated wave fixed effects and otherwise equivalent likelihood and parameterisation, within-wave IRRs and their cross-wave interaction contrasts are unchanged. Priors or constraints on wave effects can break exact equivalence. Unequal valid weeks also break it.

Person-weeks are useful for comparing intensity across unequal windows, but cannot repair missing peaks or changes in testing.

Technical notes and additional evidence

Maximum range: 0.42 (W1, 6–17, Typ 4; IRRs 0.84–1.28). Eight cells had a non-significant coarse result but a narrowly significant fine result. No fine estimate was significantly in the opposite direction. This supports a practical coarse presentation, not equivalence.

First: what does correlation establish?

The record reports a correlation of 0.996 between coarse and aggregated fine estimates. This supports similar overall patterns, not numerical equivalence or absence of subgroup heterogeneity.

Why the Q tests are limited

The Cochran Q test asks whether the fine subgroup log-IRRs within one coarse group are compatible with one common effect. It compares each estimate with their precision-weighted mean: Q = Σ wⱼ(β̂ⱼ − β̄)², with wⱼ = 1/SEⱼ². Under independent estimates and regularity conditions, Q is approximately chi-squared with number of subgroups minus one degrees of freedom. The 96 tests found no p below 0.05; minimum p = 0.07. But the same Kreise appear in all subgroups, so independence is not assured.

Reported external check (not reproduced by the report author): W5, Typ 3, 18–39, fine IRRs 1.10 / 1.05 / 1.10, coarse 1.09; Q p = 0.13, district-bootstrap covariance test p ≈ 0.0000006. Even if a difference is statistically detectable, ask whether it is large enough to matter. Positive covariance can make the independence test conservative for differences; the exact effect depends on the covariance matrix.

Estimates based on the same districts can be correlated. The variance of their difference is Var(β̂₁) + Var(β̂₂) − 2 Cov(β̂₁, β̂₂). Treating the covariance as zero may overstate or understate uncertainty.

What about GAM?

Phase 3 Part 2 (script 13) fitted a negative-binomial GAM per wave using ages 0–79; 80+ cannot be treated as a single exact age. Age main effects are factors, while the Kreistyp contrast varies smoothly with age. On 24 November, Bundesland and covariate adjustment was made age-specific. Averaged GAM curves compared with grouped m1: r = 0.90, mean absolute difference 0.026; with A1: r = 0.93, mean absolute difference 0.021 (log-IRR). The GAM checks patterns without fixed age boundaries; it is not a proof that seven groups are optimal. District bootstrap bands are preferred, and the curves still depend on smoothing assumptions.

A smooth age effect can preserve more age resolution but can also smooth abrupt transitions; boundary estimates can be uncertain. It offers complementary evidence, not proof of equivalence.

Proposed evaluation: compare matched contrasts, difference plots, maximum differences, uncertainty, cell sparsity, and prespecified subgroup contrasts using joint covariance or district-level resampling (if districts can reasonably be treated as independent). Define a meaningful tolerance before claiming practical equivalence.

Evidence: Gesamtbericht §4.4; Phase 1 “age groups laut Klima”; Phase 2 “Feines Altersschema”; Phase 3 Parts 1–3, scripts 12, 13, 15.

STEP 04

Missing values and data coverage

Which observations should enter the model when data are incomplete?

A zero case count is an observed value. A missing case count or population is not zero. Population is the denominator of the rate and the exposure in the count model.

STEP 05

Regional structural variables

Which measure should represent urbanisation, and should we combine measures?

Choosing the main regional variable

Lower conditional AIC in single-age GAM comparisons, not A1. Kreistyp describes settlement categories; density describes a continuous gradient; Lagetyp describes centrality. Better fit is relevant, but does not make these measures interchangeable.

Phase 2 found Spearman ρ = −0.81 between density and Kreistyp codes. They are related but not equivalent. The GAM already compares the 90th and 10th density percentiles (1,518 and 85 residents/km²); flexible and linear density contrasts differ on average by only 0.008–0.021 log-IRR.

To test this empirically, plot actual district densities by Kreistyp, cross-tabulate proposed density bands against the BBSR categories and quantify misclassification. High density is not automatically equivalent to Großstadt. Alternatively keep density continuous and report predicted rates at representative densities, such as the already studied 85 and 1,518 residents/km².

STEP 07

Model family and dispersion

Why is NB2 the current working choice?

Panel context. The reported Poisson and NB diagnostics concern wave-level case counts. A weekly alternative requires its own diagnostics.

Both Poisson and NB2 model expected case counts μ with a log link and an exposure offset. Poisson assumes Var(Y)=μ; NB2 allows Var(Y)=μ+μ²/θ. The parameter θ controls extra variation: smaller θ means more overdispersion at the same μ; larger θ approaches Poisson for fixed μ.

W5 · ages 18–39≈192Poisson Pearson dispersion
Type 4 contrast≈14×NB vs ordinary Poisson SE

These project results argue strongly against ordinary Poisson model-based SEs for that fit. They do not establish that NB2 is the only valid variance model, or rule out a different mean structure, Poisson mixed models or robust inference.

Age-specific dispersion. In the earlier joint model, Pearson dispersion for W5 ages 0–5 was 3.41; with A1 it was 0.99. The age-specific θ specification has strong reported fit support (LR statistics 403–1,584 across waves; boundary cases require care). This is a separate decision from the choice of random effects.

Technical notes and additional evidence

For example, μ=50 and θ=5 gives NB2 variance 550, versus 50 under Poisson. The expected count is the same, but the uncertainty differs. Use the sliders below to see how this changes with μ and θ. Quasi-Poisson is shown only as an alternative variance relationship.

NB2 is a sensible provisional choice. Check residual variation by age, convergence, and the impact of age-specific dispersion. Quasi-Poisson or robust Poisson can be targeted sensitivity analyses if useful; quasi-Poisson does not have ordinary likelihood AIC.

Evidence: Gesamtbericht §§0.2, 9; Phase 3 Parts 1, 5, 10.

Discuss together

NB2 has strong empirical support as the working family. Check whether its variance fits all age groups, and whether age-specific θ and grouping effects are needed. No need to reopen ordinary Poisson as an equally plausible final model unless a substantially different mean or random-effects structure justifies a new diagnostic.

Technical notes: grouping and dispersion

Random effects do not eliminate category-level predictions: they replace unrestricted fixed coefficients with partially pooled deviations. For our named age groups and four Kreistyp categories, fixed effects are the more direct choice; district-specific age deviations can be random in addition.

Earlier joint model shared θ across ages. W5 ages 0–5 Pearson dispersion 3.41 fell to 0.99 with A1; 80+ was also under-modelled. Rank 2 improves AIC by 228–421 in all waves and coefficients correlate 0.998 with A1, but W2 and W8 tests differ and W2 has local optima. A1 is a proposal, not a final decision.

With repeated ages and waves in the same district, observations can share unmeasured district characteristics. Dispersion and random effects address related but distinct aspects of variation.

STEP 10

Dependence and autocorrelation

Are observations still related after accounting for the fixed effects and random effects?

Random effects are one way to model dependence, but a shared Kreis effect does not automatically capture serial correlation or similarity between neighbouring Kreise.

Project evidence: m1 residual Moran’s I median 0.20; A1 retains spatial residual correlation in W2–W8 (0.04–0.24 across waves). Option III adds BYM2, but some age-specific spatial dependence remains. Option D includes cross-wave district factors and reports a three-way test p ≈ 10⁻¹⁶; its Bundesland adjustment differs from A1. No fitted weekly AR model is documented.

Chosen panel/modelWhat dependence is relevant?What can we answer?
Weekly panelWithin Kreis × age serial dependence, across ages, spatialWeekly levels and time patterns; interactions if included

Without a temporal covariance term, the mean model can still estimate the same intended associations and interactions, but uncertainty may be unreliable if dependence remains unaccounted for. Including covariance does not itself create a time interaction. Separate wave fits do not need weekly AR(1) because weekly observations have been aggregated; formal cross-wave inference still needs their joint covariance.

Technical notes: temporal covariance and calendar spacing

Wave fixed effects describe average wave differences. Interactions describe changes in associations. Temporal covariance describes remaining dependence after modelling the mean.

Temporal dependence can improve uncertainty estimates and describe residual persistence. It cannot by itself establish changing Kreistyp effects, transmission between districts, or timing of peaks. Spatial effects likewise describe shared variation, not its causal mechanism.

Fixed-effects hierarchy

How much additional complexity is required to answer our questions?

Panel context. The hierarchy below is a proposed joint-panel comparison. A1 is instead fitted separately for each wave, with 28 core fixed-effect coefficients per wave (7 age groups × 4 Kreistypen).

A = age group · P = pandemic phase · K = Kreistyp

Time specificationCore coefficientsInterpretation

The hybrid model adds 132 − 8 = 124 independent within-wave week baseline contrasts to 224. Week and wave main effects are redundant when week is nested in wave; use a full-rank parameterisation. Smooth time effects are another option. Parameter counts alone do not establish model quality or feasibility.

To test whether Kreistyp contributes at all, compare a reduced model without K and all its interactions to an appropriate full model, not just one main-effect coefficient. For a single wave use A + K vs A * K. For a joint weekly panel, a week baseline can replace P main effects while phase interactions remain possible. Hold sample, exposure, covariates, family and dependence structure constant within nested comparisons. Use LRT when valid, joint Wald tests with suitable covariance, AIC/BIC within comparable likelihoods, diagnostics and effect sizes. Do not gate interaction testing on a significant main effect.
